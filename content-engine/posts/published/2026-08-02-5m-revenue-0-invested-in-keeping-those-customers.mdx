## Variant A

$5,000,000 in revenue.

$0 spent keeping any of those customers around.

Sounds impossible. It's not. It's the default.

Here's how it happens.

You start at zero. You need customers, so you spend on ads. It works. Revenue climbs.

You spend more. Revenue climbs more. The formula seems obvious now.

Acquisition works, so acquisition gets all the attention. All the budget. All the headcount.

Nobody's job is retention. Nobody's job is the second, third, or tenth order.

Eighteen months later you're doing $5M a year and you still haven't sent a single email built to bring a customer back.

Not one flow. Not one campaign. Not one dollar.

The business runs entirely on new customers replacing the ones who never came back.

That works right up until it doesn't.

Your ad costs climb every year, because everyone's fishing in the same pond.

Your repeat purchase rate stays exactly where it started, because nothing was ever built to move it.

Margin gets squeezed from both directions. You're paying more to acquire and getting the same low return per customer.

This isn't a story about a business doing badly.

It's a story about a business doing well enough that nobody stopped to ask what happens to a customer after they buy.

If you've scaled mostly on acquisition, this is worth fifteen minutes with your numbers. Not a pitch. Just the maths.

## Variant B

Let me guess how you got to $5M.

You found a channel that worked. You scaled it hard.

You hired for growth. You hired for ads, for creative, for CRO.

Every dollar you spent had a clear job: bring in a new customer.

It worked. Revenue went from $1M to $3M to $5M.

Nobody on your team has "retention" in their title. Nobody owns what happens after the sale.

You've built a genuinely impressive acquisition machine.

You've built nothing to keep the customers it brings in.

Most founders at this stage assume retention will sort itself out. The product's good, the service is good, people will just come back.

Some do. Most don't. Not because they didn't like you. Because nobody gave them a reason to think about you again.

Here's where the maths stops being forgiving.

Acquisition costs only go one direction over time, and it's up.

Your repeat purchase rate stays flat, because nothing is designed to move it.

Every year, you need more new customers just to stand still, and each one costs more than the last.

That's not a growth problem. That's a margin problem wearing a growth costume.

The businesses that keep compounding past $5M aren't the ones who acquire the hardest.

They're the ones who worked out, early, that a customer who buys three times is worth more than three customers who buy once.
