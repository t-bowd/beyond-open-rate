You check Search Console, see traffic is up, and feel good for about four minutes. Then you look at what people actually searched to land on your site and realise none of it has anything to do with what you sell. Congratulations, your site is **ranking for the wrong keywords**, and that traffic spike is doing nothing for revenue.

This happens more than most business owners realise, and it's rarely a penalty or a technical fault. It's usually a mismatch between what a page says and what a searcher wants, and it's fixable without a rebuild.

## What "ranking for the wrong keywords" actually looks like

It's not always obvious, because the symptom is usually "traffic looks fine, enquiries don't." A few patterns to watch for:

- A product page ranks for informational searches ("what is...", "how does... work") instead of buying searches ("buy", "price", "near me").
- A service page for one offer ranks for a completely different, adjacent service you also mention on the page.
- A blog post outranks your actual sales page for a commercial term, so Google sends buyers to content that never asks for the sale.
- Your homepage ranks for your brand name and nothing else, while category pages rank for nothing at all.

For an ecommerce store, this might mean your "leather handbags" collection page ranks for "how leather is made" because of an educational paragraph you added for SEO padding. For a service business, it might mean your "bookkeeping services Melbourne" page ranks for "bookkeeping vs accounting" because that comparison section is the most substantial block of text on the page.

## Why it happens: search intent mismatch

Search engines try to match a query to the page that best satisfies the **intent** behind it, not just the keyword in the title. There are generally four flavours of intent: informational (learning), navigational (finding a specific brand or site), commercial investigation (comparing options), and transactional (ready to buy or book).

If your page mixes signals, the algorithm has to guess which intent you're serving, and it often guesses wrong. A page with a heading that says "What Is SEO?" followed by a pitch for your SEO service is sending mixed signals. It might rank for "what is SEO", an informational query that will never convert, instead of "SEO agency Sydney", the transactional query that pays your bills.

This is also why adding more content to a page doesn't always help. If you bolt an 800-word explainer onto a sales page to "help with SEO," you've just diluted the page's intent and taught Google to treat it as an educational resource.

> **Benchmark:** Google's own Search Quality Rater Guidelines explicitly separate pages by intent category (Know, Do, Website, Visit) because mismatched intent is one of the most common reasons a result disappoints a searcher, and disappointing results get demoted over time.

## How to check this in Search Console

You don't need a paid tool to diagnose this. Google Search Console's Performance report, which is free, shows you exactly which queries bring traffic to which pages.

1. Go to **Performance > Search Results** and add a **Pages** filter for the page in question.
2. Look at the **Queries** table underneath. Sort by impressions.
3. Ask one question for each top query: would someone searching this actually want what's on this page?
4. Flag anything with high impressions, low click-through rate, and a position between 5 and 20. That combination is the classic sign Google is unsure where this page belongs.

Do this for your five highest-traffic pages first. For an ecommerce store, start with your top category and product pages. For a service business, start with your main service pages and pricing page, because those are the pages that should be converting, not just ranking.

## How to retarget a page once you've found the mismatch

Once you know a page is attracting the wrong query, you've got three options, roughly in order of effort.

**Rewrite the page to match the intent you want.** If your service page keeps ranking for an informational query, strip out the educational tangent and move it to its own blog post. Make the title, H1 and first paragraph unmistakably transactional: what the service is, who it's for, what it costs, how to book.

**Split one page into two.** If a page is trying to serve both "what is X" and "buy X" searches, it's probably serving neither well. Create a dedicated article for the informational query and let the original page go hard on conversion. Link between them so the informational page passes interested readers to the commercial one.

**Redirect internal links and anchor text.** Google partly figures out what a page is about from the words other pages use to link to it. If ten pages on your site link to your pricing page with the anchor text "learn more about pricing structures," that's a soft, educational signal. Change the anchor text to something closer to what buyers search, and you nudge the page toward the right intent.

For ecommerce specifically, check your collection pages aren't ranking for the generic category term while your actual money pages (bestsellers, specific product variants) rank for nothing. For service businesses, check that your location or niche-specific pages ("SEO for law firms", "bookkeeping for tradies") aren't being outranked by your generic homepage, which usually happens when the homepage has more backlinks and more text, even though it serves none of the niche intent.

## When the fix is bigger than one page

Sometimes the mismatch isn't a single page problem, it's a site structure problem. If your blog consistently outranks your service pages for commercial terms, your site's internal linking is probably funnelling all its authority toward content instead of conversion pages. That's a sign your content and your commercial pages were built separately instead of planned together, which is the single most common SEO mistake we see when we review ecommerce and service sites.

This is also where AI-driven search changes the stakes slightly. If an AI Overview or a chatbot answer pulls from your informational page instead of your commercial one, you've lost the click before the searcher even reaches your site. Getting the commercial page right matters more now, not less, because there are fewer clicks to go around and the wrong page getting the click wastes one of them.
