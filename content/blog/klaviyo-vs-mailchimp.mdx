For years, Mailchimp owned email marketing. Then ecommerce stores started needing more than newsletters. They needed systems that understood purchase behaviour, browsed products, and customer value. That's where Klaviyo stepped in.

If you're choosing between the two in 2026, the decision isn't about features on a spreadsheet. It's about what your store actually needs to do with customer data.

## Data integration: surface-level vs structural

Mailchimp connects to Shopify, WooCommerce, and BigCommerce. It pulls in names, emails, and order totals. That's enough to send a post-purchase "thanks for buying" email.

**Klaviyo syncs every event your ecommerce platform tracks.** Product views. Cart additions. Specific items purchased. Discount codes used. Custom properties you've added to products. It treats your store as a database, not a mailing list.

Example: you sell skincare. In Mailchimp, you can segment "bought in the last 90 days". In Klaviyo, you can segment "bought the vitamin C serum, hasn't bought the night cream, browsed the retinol page twice, predicted to spend $180+ in the next six months". That level of granularity changes what emails you can send.

If you're only sending two campaigns a month and don't track customer lifetime value, Mailchimp's surface-level sync is fine. If you're optimising around repeat purchase and AOV, Klaviyo's depth matters.

## Segmentation: tags vs predictive properties

Mailchimp built its segmentation around **tags and groups**. You manually tag contacts, or set up rules based on campaign activity. It works. It's also static, you're categorising people based on what they've already done, not what they're likely to do next.

Klaviyo includes **predictive analytics** out of the box. Expected date of next order. Churn risk. Predicted lifetime value. Gender prediction based on purchase patterns (useful if you sell across categories). These aren't add-ons, they're baked into every account with enough data.

A Klaviyo segment might be: "High-risk churn, last purchase 45–60 days ago, predicted LTV above $400". You can't build that in Mailchimp without exporting to a spreadsheet and re-uploading.

For stores doing $50k/month or more, this difference compounds. The more sophisticated your segmentation, the higher your revenue per recipient.

## Automation flows: journeys vs conditional splits

Both platforms offer automation. Mailchimp calls them **Customer Journeys**. Klaviyo calls them **Flows**. The naming doesn't matter. The logic does.

Mailchimp's journey builder is visual and approachable. You can set up [a welcome series, abandoned cart, and post-purchase flow](/blog/klaviyo-flows-ecommerce-guide) in an afternoon. But once you need conditional splits based on product attributes, purchase frequency, or engagement across channels, you hit limits fast.

Klaviyo's flow builder assumes you want **conditional logic everywhere**. Did they click the product link? Send email A. Ignored it? Wait two days, send email B with a discount. Bought from the email? Exit the flow and trigger a post-purchase sequence. Bought in-store instead? Tag them and skip the next three emails.

Every split, every trigger, every wait step can reference any property in your database. If your ecommerce platform tracks it, Klaviyo can act on it.

Real-world impact: stores moving from Mailchimp to Klaviyo typically see their [abandoned cart flow](/blog/abandoned-cart-email-strategy) revenue double, not because Klaviyo's templates are magic, but because you can send the right product image, the right urgency message, and the right discount threshold to the right person.

## Email design: simplicity vs customisation

Mailchimp's email builder is easier to learn. Drag-and-drop blocks, clean templates, beginner-friendly. If you're a solo founder who needs to send a weekly update, it's faster to get something decent-looking out the door.

Klaviyo's builder is more powerful but steeper to learn. You can customise every element with CSS, pull in dynamic product blocks based on browsing history, and create emails that look different for every recipient based on their data. The trade-off is time, building a sophisticated template in Klaviyo takes longer upfront.

For most ecommerce brands, the Klaviyo approach wins. Emails that show "products you viewed" or "restock alerts for items on your wishlist" convert 4–6x better than generic campaigns. Mailchimp can do basic product recommendations, but it's clunky compared to Klaviyo's native blocks.

## Pricing: when the gap actually matters

Mailchimp starts free for 500 contacts. Klaviyo starts at $45/month for 1,500 contacts. On the surface, Mailchimp looks cheaper.

But ecommerce pricing isn't about cost per contact. It's about **revenue per email**. If Klaviyo generates $8 per email sent and Mailchimp generates $2, paying 3x more for Klaviyo is a bargain.

For stores under $20k/month in revenue, Mailchimp's lower entry price makes sense if you're just doing basic campaigns. Once you're above $50k/month and have repeat customers, Klaviyo's ROI justifies the cost within the first month.

One pricing quirk: Klaviyo counts **profiles** (anyone in your database), while Mailchimp counts **subscribers** (people who opted in). If you're tracking browsing behaviour for non-subscribers, Klaviyo's count inflates faster. Plan for it.

## SMS and push: built-in vs bolted-on

Klaviyo includes **SMS and push notifications** in the same platform. You build flows that combine email, SMS, and push based on customer preferences and behaviour. One contact record, one interface, one set of segments.

Mailchimp added SMS in 2023 as a separate product. It works, but it's not integrated the way Klaviyo's is. You can't easily say "send an SMS if they didn't open the email within six hours" without workarounds.

If you're only doing email, this doesn't matter. If you're planning to add SMS in the next 12 months, Klaviyo's unified approach saves you from duct-taping platforms together later.

## When Mailchimp still makes sense

Mailchimp isn't wrong for everyone. It's still the better choice if you're:

- **Running a content site or B2B newsletter** where ecommerce depth doesn't matter
- **Just starting out** with under 1,000 subscribers and simple needs
- **Sending occasional campaigns** without automation or lifecycle marketing
- **Working with a tight budget** and can't justify $45/month yet

If your email strategy is "send a monthly update and maybe a sale announcement", Mailchimp does that cheaper and easier.
